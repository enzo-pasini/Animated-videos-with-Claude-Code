# Facts — Why your ears pop on a plane

Checked 2026-11-04. Quotes are copied from the pages as opened; "…" marks where the page's sentence goes on
(the fetch tool returns long sentences in parts). mayoclinic.org itself refused automated access (HTTP 403), so the

## F1 — Muffled hearing and a full, stuffy feeling in the ear are common symptoms of airplane ear.
status: confirmed
- source: https://www.amerikanhastanesi.org/mayo-clinic-care-network/mayo-clinic-health-information-library/diseases-conditions/airplane-ear — Mayo Clinic Staff (via American Hospital, Mayo Clinic Care Network), last updated Oct 2026
  quote: "Feeling of fullness or stuffiness in the ear."
  quote: "Muffled hearing or some hearing loss."
- source: https://medlineplus.gov/ency/article/001064.htm — MedlinePlus (U.S. National Library of Medicine), "Ear barotrauma", reviewed 4/29/2026
  quote: "Ear barotrauma causes discomfort in the ear due to pressure differences between the inside and outside of the eardrum."

## F2 — During a flight the cabin air is kept at the pressure of about 6,000–8,000 ft; 8,000 ft is the legal ceiling.
status: confirmed
say: "as thin as on a mountain, up to eight thousand feet high"
- source: https://www.cdc.gov/yellow-book/hcp/travel-air-sea/air-travel.html — CDC Yellow Book, "Air Travel", Apr 23, 2025
  quote: "Cabin pressures are typically maintained at an equivalent of 1,830–2,440 m (approximately 6,000–8,000 ft) above sea level."
- source: https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/FR-2023-06-15/html/2023-12454.htm — FAA final rule "High Elevation Airport Operations", Federal Register 88 FR 39152, June 15, 2023 (text of 14 CFR 25.841(a))
  quote: "pressurized cabins and compartments to be occupied must be equipped to provide … a cabin pressure altitude of not more than 8,000 feet under normal operating conditions."
- source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cabin_pressurization — Wikipedia
  quote: "On commercial aircraft, the cabin altitude must be maintained at 8,000 ft (2,438 m) or less."

## F3 — Going up, the air in the middle ear expands and pushes its own way out; coming down, the tube has to be actively opened to let air back in.
status: confirmed
- source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eustachian_tube — Wikipedia
  quote: "The air in the middle ear expands as the plane gains altitude, and pushes its way into the back of the nose and mouth …"
  quote: "Active opening of the Eustachian tube (through actions like swallowing or the Valsalva maneuver) is required …" (the sentence ends: to equalize the middle-ear pressure with the cabin "as the plane descends")
- source: https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/PMC6779601/fullTextXML — Bhattacharya, Singh, Marzo, "'Airplane ear' — A neglected yet preventable problem", AIMS Public Health 6(3):320–325, 2019 (peer-reviewed)
  quote: "The eustachian tube becomes flattened during the pressure changes and it necessitates bringing air into the middle ear."

## F4 — The eustachian tube connects the middle ear to the back of the nose and throat.
status: confirmed
- source: https://medlineplus.gov/ency/article/001064.htm — MedlinePlus (U.S. National Library of Medicine)
  quote: "The eustachian tube is the connection between the middle ear and the back of the nose and upper throat."
- source: https://www.amerikanhastanesi.org/mayo-clinic-care-network/mayo-clinic-health-information-library/diseases-conditions/airplane-ear — Mayo Clinic Staff (via American Hospital)
  quote: "A narrow passage called the eustachian tube connects the middle ear to the back of the nose and throat."

## F5 — The eustachian tube is very narrow: about the width of a pencil lead (about 3 mm across in adults).
status: confirmed
say: "about as wide as a pencil lead"
- source: https://www.enthealth.org/conditions/ears-and-altitude-barotrauma/ — American Academy of Otolaryngology–Head and Neck Surgery Foundation (ENThealth), "Ears and Altitude (Barotrauma)", reviewed Aug 2018
  quote: "a membrane-lined tube about the width of a pencil lead"
- source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eustachian_tube — Wikipedia
  quote: "In adult humans, the Eustachian tube is approximately 35 mm (1.4 in) long and 3 mm (0.12 in) in diameter."

## F6 — The eustachian tube is normally closed (collapsed) and gapes open when you swallow.
status: confirmed
- source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eustachian_tube — Wikipedia
  quote: "Normally, the Eustachian tube is collapsed, but it gapes open with swallowing …"
- source: https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/PMC6779601/fullTextXML — AIMS Public Health, 2019 (peer-reviewed)
  quote: "The eustachian tube becomes flattened during the pressure changes and it necessitates bringing air into the middle ear."

## F7 — Swallowing or yawning opens the tube (throat muscles pull it open), so air flows in or out and the pressure evens out.
status: confirmed
- source: https://medlineplus.gov/ency/article/001064.htm — MedlinePlus (U.S. National Library of Medicine)
  quote: "Swallowing or yawning opens the eustachian tube and allows air to flow into or out of the middle ear."
  quote: "This helps equalize pressure on either side of the ear drum."
- source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eustachian_tube — Wikipedia
  quote: "The tube is opened during swallowing by contraction of the tensor veli palatini and levator veli palatini …"
- source: https://www.amerikanhastanesi.org/mayo-clinic-care-network/mayo-clinic-health-information-library/diseases-conditions/airplane-ear — Mayo Clinic Staff (via American Hospital)
  quote: "Swallowing or yawning opens the eustachian tube."

## F8 — When a plane climbs or comes down, the cabin air pressure changes fast (it rises on the way down).
status: confirmed
- source: https://www.amerikanhastanesi.org/mayo-clinic-care-network/mayo-clinic-health-information-library/diseases-conditions/airplane-ear — Mayo Clinic Staff (via American Hospital)
  quote: "When an airplane climbs or goes lower, the air pressure changes fast."
- source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cabin_pressurization — Wikipedia
  quote: "… passengers may experience discomfort or acute pain as gases trapped within their bodies expand or contract."

## F9 — When the pressure outside is higher than in the middle ear, the eardrum is pulled (bent) inward.
status: confirmed
say: "your eardrums bend inward" (it happens when the ear hasn't equalized yet)
- source: https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/PMC6779601/fullTextXML — AIMS Public Health, 2019 (peer-reviewed)
  quote: "the tympanic membrane may be pulled inside due to the pressure gradient."
- source: https://medlineplus.gov/ency/article/001064.htm — MedlinePlus (U.S. National Library of Medicine)
  quote: "the health care provider may see a slight outward bulge or inward pull of the eardrum."

## F10 — When the tube opens and the pressure evens out, you hear a small pop.
status: confirmed
- source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eustachian_tube — Wikipedia
  quote: "When this happens, humans hear a small popping sound, an event familiar to aircraft passengers …"
- source: https://www.enthealth.org/conditions/ears-and-altitude-barotrauma/ — AAO-HNS Foundation (ENThealth)
  quote: "The simplest way to help clear your ears—particularly when flying—is to swallow."

## F11 — Chewing gum helps because it makes you swallow more easily.
status: confirmed
- source: https://www.amerikanhastanesi.org/mayo-clinic-care-network/mayo-clinic-health-information-library/diseases-conditions/airplane-ear — Mayo Clinic Staff (via American Hospital)
  quote: "Sucking on candy or chewing gum can make swallowing easier."
- source: https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/PMC6779601/fullTextXML — AIMS Public Health, 2019 (peer-reviewed)
  quote: "chewing gum and swallowing activate the muscles that make patent the eustachian tubes bi-laterally."
