# Facts — The Ghosts From the Furnace

The story (Lena, her family, the priest, the repairman) is invented and tagged `[story]`. The facts below are the real case and the real mechanism.

## F1 — In 1921 a doctor (ophthalmologist W. H. Wilmer) published in a medical journal a real family's "haunting": footsteps, figures at night, dead houseplants, all caused by a faulty furnace leaking carbon monoxide.
status: confirmed
- source: https://www.iflscience.com/100yearold-haunted-house-mystery-solved-by-professor-and-ophthalmologist-59607 — IFLScience (James Felton), May 6, 2021
  quote: "Wilmer described a haunting of a whole family, who began to see and hear strange people moving about in the night after they moved into an old, run-down house."
  quote: "He found the furnace in a very bad condition, the combustion being imperfect, the fumes, instead of going up the chimney, were pouring gases of carbon monoxid into our rooms."
  quote: "It didn't help calm things down when the family investigated and found that the previous occupants had had the same experiences as they had, nor when all the plants in the house died – though both gave almighty big clues as to what was going on."
- source: https://ulse.org/ul-standards-engagement/carbon-monoxide-safety/ghosts-ghouls-and-faulty-furnaces-these-things-go — UL Standards & Engagement (safety nonprofit), October 31, 2024
  quote: "G and his family is real, and was detailed in 1921 in an article by W.H."
  quote: "There were several reports of disembodied footsteps on the hardwood where carpeting was laid, distorted faces staring back from the mirror in the evening, well-dressed apparitions that came and went, and even full hallucinations of the fire department approaching."
- source: https://www.todayifoundout.com/?p=51229 — Today I Found Out
  quote: "But perhaps his most unusual claim to fame is the fact that in 1921 he managed to talk a prestigious medical journal, The American Journal of Ophthalmology, into printing a ghost story."
  quote: "H took the doctor’s advice and moved out of the house until the furnace could be repaired."

Note: the original paper is W. H. Wilmer, "Effects of Carbon Monoxid upon the Eye", American Journal of Ophthalmology 4 (1921): 73–90. Its full text was not opened; three independent retellings agree on the core (1921, the journal, the furnace, footsteps and figures, dead plants). The script only says "a doctor reported a real family just like Lena's".

## F2 — A broken furnace can send its fumes into the house instead of up the chimney; furnaces are a household source of carbon monoxide.
status: confirmed
- source: https://www.cdc.gov/carbon-monoxide/about/index.html — U.S. Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC), "Carbon Monoxide Poisoning Basics"
  quote: "Many household items including gas- and oil-burning furnaces, portable generators, and charcoal grills produce this poison gas."
- source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carbon_monoxide_poisoning — Wikipedia, "Carbon monoxide poisoning"
  quote: "Sources of carbon monoxide include cigarette smoke, house fires, faulty furnaces, heaters, wood-burning stoves, internal combustion vehicle exhaust, electrical generators, propane-fueled equipment such as portable stoves, and gasoline-powered tools such as leaf blowers, lawn mowers, high-pressure washers, concrete cutting saws, power trowels, and welders."

## F3 — Carbon monoxide has no color and no smell.
status: confirmed
- source: https://www.cdc.gov/carbon-monoxide/about/index.html — CDC
  quote: "What it is Carbon monoxide (CO) is an odorless, colorless gas that kills without warning."
- source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carbon_monoxide_poisoning — Wikipedia
  quote: "CO is a colorless and odorless gas which is initially non-irritating."

## F4 — Carbon monoxide sticks to hemoglobin (the oxygen carrier in blood) about 200 times more strongly than oxygen, so the blood carries less oxygen.
status: confirmed
say: "about two hundred times"
- source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carbon_monoxide_poisoning — Wikipedia
  quote: "In the blood stream carbon monoxide has an affinity with hemoglobin some 200 times greater than oxygen."
  quote: "In humans, the affinity between hemoglobin and carbon monoxide is approximately 240 times stronger than the affinity between hemoglobin and oxygen."
  quote: "Carbon monoxide primarily causes adverse effects by combining with hemoglobin to form carboxyhemoglobin (symbol COHb or HbCO), which prevents the blood from carrying oxygen and expelling carbon dioxide as carbaminohemoglobin."

Note: sources give 200 to 240 times; the script says "about two hundred times" (the lower, safer number).

## F5 — Carbon monoxide poisoning causes headache, tiredness and confusion, and with more exposure hallucinations.
status: softened
say: "a brain short of oxygen **can** hear and see things that aren't there"; headaches and tiredness said plainly.
- source: https://www.cdc.gov/carbon-monoxide/about/index.html — CDC
  quote: "Symptoms The most common symptoms of CO poisoning are headache, dizziness, weakness, upset stomach, vomiting, chest pain, and confusion."
- source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carbon_monoxide_poisoning — Wikipedia
  quote: "Increasing exposure produces cardiac abnormalities including fast heart rate, low blood pressure, and cardiac arrhythmia; central nervous system symptoms include delirium, hallucinations, dizziness, unsteady gait, confusion, seizures, central nervous system depression, unconsciousness, respiratory arrest, and death."
  quote: "The initial symptoms of acute carbon monoxide poisoning include headache, nausea, malaise, and fatigue."
- source: https://ulse.org/ul-standards-engagement/carbon-monoxide-safety/ghosts-ghouls-and-faulty-furnaces-these-things-go — UL Standards & Engagement
  quote: "It can cause hallucinations, confusion, and even death."

Note: softened to "can" because hallucinations come with heavier exposure, not in every case.
